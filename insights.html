Denials

Why claims get denied, and where to stop them

PRISCO editorial team · 5 minute read

A denial is rarely a billing problem. It is usually a problem that started earlier and showed up at the end. Sorting denials by where they began shows which fixes will pay off.

1. Front-end denials

These begin at registration and scheduling: coverage inactive on the date of service, the wrong plan on file, a procedure performed without the required prior authorization. They are the most preventable denials because the information existed before the visit.

  • Verify eligibility before the visit, not after the claim.
  • Attach authorization numbers to the right encounter and date.
  • Check registration data against the insurance card.

2. Coding and documentation denials

Here the claim does not match the chart: a missing modifier, an E/M level the note does not support, a diagnosis that does not justify the procedure, or two codes that NCCI edits say cannot be billed together without a modifier.

Example. A visit billed with a minor procedure on the same day can deny the visit unless the record shows a significant, separately identifiable service and the claim carries the right modifier.
  • Build each code from the documentation, then audit a sample every week.
  • Return error types to coders as trends, not as individual corrections.
  • Use a query when the note is unclear. Never guess to make a code fit.

3. Back-end denials

Timely filing misses, late appeals, and the same denial reason repeating month after month because nobody feeds the cause back upstream.

  • Work denials by value and deadline, with a named owner for each.
  • Group denials by cause and payer every month.
  • Send each root cause to the team that can remove it, then check that it stopped.

What to measure

Track the share of denials that were preventable, not only the total denial rate. A falling preventable share tells you the fixes are working upstream.

Coding

Code-set updates and your denial rate

PRISCO editorial team · 4 minute read

Code sets change on a fixed calendar. Teams that plan for it see a short dip in claim quality. Teams that do not see denials for weeks.

The calendar

  • October 1: ICD-10-CM and ICD-10-PCS updates take effect with the new federal fiscal year.
  • January 1: CPT updates take effect, along with new HCPCS Level II codes.
  • Every quarter: NCCI procedure-to-procedure edits are updated by CMS, and HCPCS Level II codes can change.

What goes wrong

  • Discontinued codes still sit in charge masters, templates and superbills.
  • Coders use an old code book or an encoder that has not been updated.
  • New edits start rejecting code pairs that used to pass.

A simple update routine

  • Two weeks before each effective date, list the changes that touch your specialties.
  • Update charge masters, templates and scrubber rules before go-live.
  • Brief coders with real examples, then audit the first two weeks at a higher sample rate.
  • Watch rejections and denials by code for 30 days after each change.

Vendor selection

What a pilot audit should tell you

PRISCO editorial team · 4 minute read

A good pilot answers one question: would you trust this team with your live charts? Judge it by what comes back, not by how it was pitched.

You choose the charts

A pilot on charts the vendor selects proves little. Pick charts from the specialties and payers that cause you the most trouble.

The report should include

  • An accuracy score, with how it was calculated
  • Errors grouped by type: code selection, modifiers, sequencing, units, documentation support
  • Severity: which errors would change payment or compliance risk
  • Missed or unsupported diagnoses
  • Clear, specific fixes your own team can act on

Questions to ask afterwards

  • Who reviewed the charts, and what are their credentials?
  • Was there a second reviewer?
  • How would errors be reported to you each week in live work?
  • What target would you commit to in writing?

Red flags

Promises of zero errors, no explanation of method, and reports that list only totals. Real audits find things, and the useful ones explain them.
